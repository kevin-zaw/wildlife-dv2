Millions of eyes

Every photo of a frog, bird or mushroom shared by an ordinary person can become a scientific record. Australia's national biodiversity database now holds … records of wildlife seen in 2025, and … in every 100 of them came from members of the public.

A data story by KEVIN ZAW, published October 2026. FIT3179 Data Visualisation, Monash University.

A wildlife record is one sighting: a photo, sound recording or note saying what was seen, where and when. Records count sightings, not animals, so one kookaburra photographed by ten people makes ten records. Every record in this story comes from the Atlas of Living Australia (ALA), the national biodiversity database hosted by CSIRO. This story asks four questions: who is recording wildlife, where, what they find, and when. It ends with one example of those records at work.

Who is watching?

Most wildlife records once came from museum collections and government surveys. Phone apps now let anyone contribute. There were … times as many records of wildlife seen in 2025 as for 2015. Two apps now supply most of them: …, where birdwatchers log checklists, and iNaturalist Australia, where anyone can post a photo for others to identify.

Where do people look?

Wildlife lives everywhere, but observers do not. Most people live near the coast, so that is where most sightings happen. Comparing raw totals would mostly show where people live, so the first map divides each state's records by its population.

A pale square does not mean little lives there. It means few people looked. Records from these thinly covered areas are the most valuable to scientists, because they show where species live beyond our cities.

What do they find?

Birds are easy to spot, easy to name and followed by many keen birdwatchers who log whole checklists at a time, so they dominate the records. Insects, fungi and plants make up most of Australia's species, yet they are recorded far less often. What we record says as much about us as about what lives here.

When do they look?

Records rise and fall with the seasons. Australia's seasons are the reverse of Europe's and North America's: summer runs from December to February, and spring from September to November. Birds are recorded all year, while frogs, insects and fungi each have their own busy months.

Science in action: tracking the cane toad

Cane toads were released in Queensland in 1935 to control beetles in sugar cane. They failed to control the beetles and instead spread across northern Australia. The toads are poisonous to many native predators, so knowing where they have reached helps land managers protect wildlife. Many of the location records come from members of the public.

Your turn

You don't need to be a scientist to help. Photograph a plant, insect or bird and upload it to iNaturalist, record frog calls with the FrogID app, or log the birds in your backyard with eBird. Checked records flow into the Atlas of Living Australia, where researchers can use them. The empty squares on the map are waiting.

About the data

  • Year. 2025 is the latest complete year. Records can be added months after a sighting, so totals may still grow slightly.
  • Quality. Counts use the ALA's standard data-quality profile, which leaves out records that fail its automatic checks, such as impossible locations.
  • Privacy and sensitive species. Only totals were downloaded, never individual records. The ALA already blurs the locations of threatened species.
  • Community records. Each data source was classed as community (for example iNaturalist, eBird, FrogID) or institutional (museums, herbaria, government and research surveys).
  • Maps. All maps use an Albers equal-area conic projection (standard parallels 18°S and 36°S), which keeps areas true so regions can be compared fairly.
  • Grid and cities. Grid squares are 1° of latitude by 1° of longitude (about 100 km). City totals count records within 50 km of the city centre.
